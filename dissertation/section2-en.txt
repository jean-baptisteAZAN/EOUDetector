2.1 The turn problem inside a conversation between two humans

Turn-taking is the way two speakers alternate in holding the floor. For a machine, the
difficulty is to decide, at every short pause in the exchange, whether to start speaking
because the turn has ended, or to keep waiting because the other party has not finished.
That decision rests on an uncomfortable trade-off: ending too early cuts the person off,
while waiting too long delays the reply and makes the exchange feel unnatural. No single
setting removes both errors at once. In most dialogue systems the decision is taken from
silence alone: the system waits for a pause of fixed length and then treats the turn as
over. The organisation of turn-taking has been studied since Sacks, Schegloff and
Jefferson (1974), and the rule is simple and fast, but it conflates two very different
situations - a genuine end of turn and a pause in the middle of an utterance - because
both present themselves as silence. In medical telephony this confusion is both frequent
and costly: a patient spelling a name or dictating a number produces silences that are not
ends of turn.

These are precisely the cases on which models that listen only to sound fail. Having no
access to the meaning of the words, they cannot separate a finished sentence from a
spelling sequence or a number still in progress. A perceptual study on French supports
this (Uro et al., 2024). Inter-pause units drawn from the REPERE corpus were annotated for
terminality and presented to participants either as audio or as text, with the end of the
unit truncated by up to three prosodic words; participants then judged whether the floor
could be taken. Audio and lexical cues did not carry the same information: the audio
condition allowed a turn end to be anticipated at least three words before it arrived, the
text condition considerably less. It is this complementarity of the two modalities that
motivates adding a lexical signal to the acoustic one, and it structures the review of
tools and work that follows.


2.2 Tools and technologies

The system reuses several existing components, described briefly here.

Voice activity detection is handled by Silero VAD (Silero Team, 2024), an open-source
package with no accompanying academic publication, which labels each short audio frame as
speech or silence. It is the entry point of the pipeline: it triggers an evaluation at
every transition from speech to silence.

The acoustic end-of-turn estimate comes from Smart Turn v3, a model released by Pipecat
and Daily (Pipecat AI, 2026), likewise without an academic publication and documented only
by its model card and a technical blog post. It uses the encoder layers of Whisper Tiny
(Radford et al., 2023) with a single linear classification head on top, for roughly eight
million parameters. From the recent audio it produces a probability that the turn has
ended, without reading any text. This project uses version 3.2 in its CPU export; versions
3.1 and 3.2 improved the handling of short utterances and robustness to noise, and the
exact version is stated because the reported figures depend on it.

The lexical estimate comes from CamemBERT (Martin et al., 2020), a French BERT-style
language model applied to the partial transcript. Unlike the acoustic signal, it judges
completeness from the meaning of the words. Continuous transcription is provided by a
commercial speech recognition service (Azure), which emits partial hypotheses as speech
unfolds.

Evaluation relies on eot-bench (LiveKit, 2026), an open benchmark and its dataset: the
first open collection of genuine human-to-agent turns with aligned audio and textual
context, covering fourteen languages, of which French is one subset. Rather than fixing a
single threshold, the harness sweeps the endpointing policy jointly over threshold, action
delay and timeout, and reports the resulting frontier between latency and false cutoffs.
It is an industrial artefact - a repository and a leaderboard under the Apache-2.0
licence, released in June 2026 alongside the LiveKit Turn Detector v1 - rather than a
peer-reviewed publication. Its timing matters to this project: it appeared three weeks
before implementation began, and before it there was no public benchmark of real
human-to-agent turns on which a French endpointing method could be compared with anything
else.

One methodological caveat about this benchmark should be stated at the outset, because it
qualifies the results of Chapter 5. Its authors deliberately omit scalar ranking metrics
such as AUC from their comparison reports, treating them as diagnostic only, and present
the policy sweep as the metric of record. This dissertation nonetheless reports per-span
AUC as its headline figure, for a specific reason: the question asked here is whether a
lexical signal improves the underlying score, and a policy sweep conflates score quality
with the timing policy applied on top of it. The two views are reported side by side
throughout, and where they disagree the disagreement is discussed rather than hidden.


2.3 Calibration and cost-sensitive decision

Two bodies of method underpin the decision layer of this project, and neither is specific
to turn-taking.

The first is probability calibration. A classifier is calibrated when, among the cases it
assigns a probability of 0.8, roughly eight in ten are positive. Modern neural networks
are systematically miscalibrated, and typically overconfident; Guo et al. (2017) attribute
this to increased model capacity and to training choices such as batch normalisation and
weight decay, and show that accuracy and calibration can move in opposite directions.
Calibration is therefore not implied by good discrimination: a model can rank cases
correctly, and so obtain a high AUC, while the probability values it emits are meaningless
as frequencies. The standard remedies are post-hoc and monotonic. Platt scaling (Platt,
1999) fits a sigmoid to the scores, which is parsimonious but assumes a particular
functional form; isotonic regression (Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002) fits an arbitrary
non-decreasing step function, which is more flexible but needs more held-out data and can
overfit on small sets; Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (2005) compare the two empirically
across learners. Because both mappings are monotonic, neither changes the ranking of
predictions, and therefore neither changes the AUC: they correct the values, not the
ordering. Calibration quality is measured by the expected calibration error (Naeini et
al., 2015; Guo et al., 2017), the weighted average gap between claimed confidence and
observed accuracy across probability bins.

The second is cost-sensitive decision making. Once a probability is trustworthy, the
threshold at which to act is not a free parameter but a consequence of the relative cost
of the two errors: Elkan (2001) shows that under an asymmetric loss the optimal decision
threshold follows directly from the cost ratio. This is the classical result that lets an
endpointing threshold be derived from how much worse an interruption is than a delay,
rather than being set by hand. The myopic threshold used here decides on the evidence
available at one instant. The richer alternative treats the decision as a sequential one,
in which observations accumulate and the choice is between acting now and paying to
observe more; the canonical formulation is Wald's sequential probability ratio test (Wald,
1945), and it is the framing that Chapter 6 returns to as future work.

A third strand is relevant to the in-domain dataset rather than the decision. Weak
supervision replaces manual annotation with cheap, noisy labelling heuristics (Zhou,
2018), an approach systematised by Ratner et al. (2017). It buys scale and, in a clinical
setting, avoids a person having to listen to call content, at the cost of label noise that
bounds the signal any model can recover from the data. That bound is not a detail here: it
is the explanation of the in-domain result in Section 5.7.


2.4 Related work on end-of-turn detection

Work on end-of-turn detection can be grouped by the signals it uses.

PURELY ACOUSTIC approaches decide from sound, prosody and silence, without reading the
words. They are fast and independent of transcription, but blind to meaning, and so cut
off utterances that are grammatically incomplete. Smart Turn, the baseline of this
project, belongs to this family.

PURELY LEXICAL approaches read the transcribed text and judge whether the utterance is
complete. They supply the meaning the acoustic signal lacks, but they inherit a dependency
that deserves to be stated plainly rather than in passing, because it shapes both the
design of Chapter 3 and the failure reported in Section 5.7. A lexical branch is only as
good as the transcript it reads, and a streaming transcript is late, unstable and
imperfect: partial hypotheses arrive behind the audio, they are revised as more context
arrives, and word error rates degrade on narrowband telephone speech relative to the
wideband audio on which recognisers are usually benchmarked. A system that waits for a
stable transcript buys accuracy with latency, which is the resource endpointing is trying
to save; a system that reads whatever is available reads a text that may be wrong. Neither
horn is free, and the choice between them is a design decision, not a detail of
implementation.

JOINT approaches process audio and text together. FastTurn (Wang et al., 2026) unifies
streaming CTC decoding with acoustic features so that a decision can be taken early from
partial observations while semantic cues are preserved; it performs well but is heavy and
presupposes joint training of its components. JAL-Turn (Yang et al., 2026) combines
pre-trained acoustic representations with linguistic features through a lightweight joint
model built on a frozen ASR encoder, so that it runs in parallel with recognition and adds
no latency; unlike FastTurn it does not require the two modalities to be retrained
together, and its labels are extracted automatically from large-scale dialogue corpora.
X2-Turn (Fu et al., 2026) places a frame-synchronous turn state head alongside the ASR
head on shared streaming features, and DualTurn (Rajaa, 2026) goes further still, learning
turn dynamics implicitly from dual-channel generative pre-training without turn labels at
all. Phoenix-VAD (2025) takes the opposite route to the one adopted here, placing a large
language model in the endpoint decision itself.

On the benchmarking side, the LiveKit detector and test set provide the policy-sweep
evaluation framework this project adopts. Other work remains purely acoustic: endpoint
anticipation (Udupa et al., 2026) bypasses ASR to predict a turn ending in advance, up to
roughly 2.5 seconds ahead, so that the rest of the pipeline can be started sooner. Its
object is not the choice of a decision threshold but early prediction, and the cost it
seeks to reduce is the speculative computation wasted when the pipeline is launched too
early and then abandoned.

The closest neighbour to this work is Easy Turn (Li et al., 2025), and the comparison is
worth making explicitly. Easy Turn is an open-source modular turn-taking detector that
also integrates acoustic and linguistic information, and it too positions itself against
Smart Turn. Beyond that the two diverge on every axis that matters here. Easy Turn trains:
its model is fitted on a purpose-built 1,145-hour speech corpus, and it predicts one of
four dialogue states - complete, incomplete, backchannel and wait - from which a decision
is taken by argmax. The present work trains nothing beyond a four-parameter fusion, keeps
both base models frozen and off the shelf, and emits a single scalar probability rather
than a class. Easy Turn reports accuracy on its own released test set; this work reports
on a third-party multilingual benchmark, on a language subset it did not choose. And
neither Easy Turn nor any other system reviewed here reports whether the probability it
emits is calibrated, or connects its operating point to a stated cost. The difference is
therefore not that fusion is done here and not there - it is done in both - but that the
output is treated as a probability that must be validated before a threshold placed on it
can mean anything.

It should be said plainly that the fusion mechanism itself - combining an acoustic signal
with a lexical one - and the latency-versus-false-cutoff framing are not new. This project
claims to have invented neither. It is worth quantifying how active the field is: every one
of the eight systems in Table 2.1 was published in the eleven months to August 2026, and
the most recent of them appeared three weeks before this chapter was written.


2.5 The gap addressed

Three gaps emerge from this review, and it is worth being explicit about when they were
identified. I chose this project and had it approved in April 2026; at that point, to the
best of my knowledge, no readily available system combined a lexical signal with an
acoustic one for end-of-turn detection. Almost all of the work reviewed above appeared
during the months that followed, and the LiveKit detector and its benchmark arrived in June
2026. The gaps set out below are therefore those that remain AFTER that shift, not the ones
the project was originally scoped against.

First, none of the systems reviewed reports an
explicit calibration of its probability, neither an expected calibration error nor any
recalibration, and none relates its decision threshold to a cost of interruption against
latency: eot-bench sweeps thresholds and some vendors expose confidence scores, but the
probability itself is neither measured nor corrected, so a chosen threshold has no precise
meaning. Second, the lightweight composition of off-the-shelf models, with no joint
training and no language model in the decision loop, is little studied; the trend visible
in Table 2.1 runs the other way, towards larger jointly trained systems. Third, the French
medical telephony domain, where semantically incomplete utterances - spelling, digits,
dates - are both frequent and consequential, is not addressed. These are the three gaps
this dissertation sets out to close.
