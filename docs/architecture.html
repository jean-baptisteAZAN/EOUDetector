Towards Semantically-Aware End-of-Utterance Detection.

Deciding when a caller has finished is a cost decision, not a timeout. We fuse a waveform and a half-formed sentence into a calibrated completion probability, then stop optimally.

domain French medical telephony · signals acoustic + lexical · runtime real-time, CPU, LLM-free

The interruption problem

A voice-activity detector hears silence and assumes the turn is over, so the agent talks over a caller who is mid-name, mid-number, or mid-thought. A fixed silence timeout can only trade one failure for the other: cut too early, or wait too long.

  • Spelling a name. Letters arrive with gaps between them. Example: …c'est m a r▮ cut
  • Numbers & dates. Dictated in bursts, not one breath. Example: …née le douze▮ cut
  • Thinking pauses. A trailing connector: not done. Example: …donc je▮ cut

What's new here

Fusing acoustic and lexical cues for turn detection is an active area (FastTurn, JAL-Turn, 2026). The mechanism isn't the contribution, the decision is: existing systems classify hold/shift against a learned or fixed threshold. We reframe endpointing itself.

Prior work (2026)

  • Acoustic + semantic fusion → hold/shift classifier
  • Learned or fixed decision threshold (ad-hoc)
  • Often an LLM in the loop; joint model trained end-to-end
  • English / generic; uncalibrated confidence

cf. FastTurn · JAL-Turn

contribution = the stopping rule + calibration, not the fusion

Contribution · one line. Frame semantic end-of-utterance detection as optimal stopping: a calibrated completion probability (acoustic + lexical) drives a cost-sensitive stopping rule that provably dominates fixed and learned thresholds on the latency / false-cut trade-off.

Endpointing as optimal stopping

At every micro-pause, don't check a threshold: weigh the cost of acting now against the cost of waiting, and stop when acting wins. In a medical call, cutting off a patient costs far more than a little latency, so the rule is principled, not tuned.

decision at each pause t
    stop  ⇔  E[cost | stop] ≤ E[cost | wait]

expected cost of stopping
    = (1 - p̂_eou) · C_cut
      + latency(t) · C_lat

p̂_eou = calibrated fusion(p_ac, p_lex)
  • Calibrated probability. p̂eou must mean what it says: a prerequisite the existing models skip. Calibration is a contribution in itself.
  • Cost model, not magic numbers. Ccut ≫ Clat encodes the safety-critical asymmetry; the threshold falls out of it.
  • Anticipation. A confident stop fires on the shortest pause; uncertainty buys more listening, up to a hard ceiling.
  • Bilateral veto feeds the probability: either branch strongly "not done" (spelling, open number) pulls p̂eou down.

How we prove "better"

One claim, one curve. The stopping rule traces an operating frontier on latency vs false-cut rate; we show it Pareto-dominates every baseline: lower interruptions at equal latency, or lower latency at equal interruptions.

  • VAD + fixed timeout: the incumbent; one point, one compromise
  • Smart Turn only: acoustic completion
  • CamemBERT only: lexical completion
  • Optimal stopping: a frontier that dominates them all

How it's realised

Silero VAD gates the stream and fires the decision at each micro-pause. Two off-the-shelf branches feed the calibrated probability; nothing on the decision path waits for the ASR.

  • acoustic · p_ac
  • lexical · p_lex
  • calibrated p̂_eou → stop rule
  • veto · hold

    v

  → p_ac                          → p_lex

                    |

        [ fuse + calibrate ]   p̂_eou
        veto → probability   (calibrated)
                    |
                    v
        [ decision ]   Stop rule
        cost-sensitive   WAIT / STOP

Lexical is read as a snapshot of the newest ASR partial, never an await, so the decision is bounded by one acoustic inference (~25-80 ms), never by ASR lag. Every stage sits behind an interface; the rule/logistic fusion swaps for a learned one with no caller change.
